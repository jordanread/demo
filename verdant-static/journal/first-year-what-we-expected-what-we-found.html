Four Years In: What We Expected, What We Found

A collective reflection from the founding members on the gaps between our original assumptions and what running an experimental robotics homestead actually looks like

Cassius and I started talking about this project in 2019, during a period when we were both living and working in ways that felt increasingly disconnected from things that mattered to us. We had a vague vision: a community of technically-skilled people applying those skills to regenerative land use, operating transparently, sharing what they learned. The specifics took years to fill in.

In July 2020, seven of us moved onto the property. In October 2024, we're 40 people, plus seasonal members, plus a visitor program, plus an increasingly active online community following our work. I want to mark that four-year point honestly — not with a press release, but with an accounting.

What We Expected: Technology Would Be the Hard Part

When Cassius, Tobias, and I were doing early planning, we spent most of our time on technical questions. What's the soil type? How much solar capacity? What robotics stack? What communication protocols? The assumption was that getting the technology right would be the defining challenge.

We were wrong in a specific way: the technology is hard, but it is tractable in the way that engineering problems are tractable. You can iterate on a robot. You can retrain a model. You can replace a sensor that fails. The progress is visible and the feedback loops are short.

The community dynamics have been harder, in ways that are less visible and have longer feedback loops. Forty people living and working together, making collective decisions about resources and direction and conflict — this is where most of our real difficulty has lived. Not in the soil sensors. In the meeting where two people who both care deeply about the farm disagree about how to use it, and both of them are right.

What We Expected: Clear Separation Between Technology Experiments and Community Life

We imagined a fairly clean division: the farm is managed via automation and human oversight; the community lives alongside that with normal social structures. In practice, the technology decisions and the community decisions are inseparable.

When we decided to automate the coop rotation, we were also deciding that two members' daily task of moving the coop manually was no longer needed. That sounds like an efficiency improvement; it was also a disruption to the routine those members had structured their mornings around, and an implicit statement about what kinds of contributions the community values. We didn't think about it that way before we deployed the system. We had to think about it afterward.

We now have a governance principle we didn't have at the start: any automation that eliminates a meaningful community task requires a community decision, not just a technical decision. The criteria aren't just efficiency — they include asking what the task was doing for the people doing it.

What We Found: The Documentation Became as Important as the Experiments

Anouk joined in 2021 with the explicit brief of making sure we documented our experiments properly. We thought this was a supporting function — a way to capture knowledge for internal use and share it externally.

It turned out to be core. The discipline of writing down what we were doing, why, and what happened has changed the quality of our thinking about experiments. You can't write a clear account of a poorly designed experiment without noticing the problems. The documentation process improved the experiments by requiring us to articulate them precisely.

The public journal has also created relationships with people and communities we'd never have met otherwise. Researchers who share data with us. Other communities who are running parallel experiments and want to compare notes. Students who read our failure reports and reach out. The openness has given back more than it cost.

What We Found: 40 People Is Its Own Kind of System

One of the pleasures of this work, which I didn't fully anticipate, is watching the community develop its own emergent properties. Patterns that nobody designed. Relationships that the governance documents don't capture. Informal expertise-sharing that happens in the kitchen at breakfast.

We have members who arrived as robotics engineers and became passionate composters. Members who arrived as permaculture designers and taught themselves embedded systems. The crossover is real and it changes how people approach problems — an engineer who has spent a season composting thinks about biological system failure modes differently than one who hasn't.

The Honest Accounting

Things that have gone worse than expected: community growth has been harder to manage than we anticipated. We've gotten better at it but the learning curve for new members is steep, and we've had people leave who didn't fit, which is always painful. The technology has also been more expensive than initial projections — hardware costs, failure rates, the labor of skilled people maintaining complex systems.

Things that have gone better than expected: food production yields are significantly above our projections. The knowledge-sharing culture has been stronger and more generative than we hoped. The external relationships — with researchers, other communities, visitors — have exceeded what we imagined for a project this size.

Things we still don't know: whether any of this is replicable. Our specific combination of people, place, skills, and timing may not generalize to a model that others can copy. We hope it does. We're trying to document it carefully enough that people can try. But we're aware that we might just be lucky in ways we don't fully understand.

Come visit. Look at the actual soil. Talk to actual people. Ask hard questions. That's the best documentation we have.
