How this started

The argument that started everything.

In the winter of 2019, Cassius Wade and Nkechi Obi-Solomon were having a recurring argument about automation. Cassius believed that the tools of industrial robotics could be adapted for small-scale regenerative agriculture in ways that researchers and companies weren't pursuing seriously. Nkechi believed that even if the technology worked, you'd need a fundamentally different kind of organization to use it well.

They were both right. In early 2020, they put out a call to people working on adjacent problems — roboticists, soil scientists, permaculture designers, fabricators — and proposed something unusual: come live on a farm and build this together.

Seven people moved onto the property in Albemarle County in July 2020. The first season, most of the technology was aspirational. Three serious disagreements in the first six months. Facilitated resolution processes we hadn't built yet. We broke things we couldn't afford to break and fixed them anyway.

Four years later: 40 members, 9 autonomous systems, 18 acres under active management, and a governance structure that has held up through everything. We don't think we've figured this out. We think we're getting somewhere.

Mission
"The Verdant Forge Collective exists to explore, document, and share what happens when you apply serious technical capability to regenerative agriculture — operated by the people who live with the results."
01

Radical transparency

We document what doesn't work at least as carefully as what does. Failure is information. Unpublished failure is waste.

02

Biology first

Every technical decision gets tested against: is this good for the soil, the plants, and the animals? Efficiency that degrades biological health is not efficiency.

03

Open by default

All designs, code, models, and data are published under open licenses. If we've figured something out, other people should be able to use it.

04

Human judgment in the loop

Automation makes tasks consistent. Human judgment makes them right. We do not build systems that remove people from consequential decisions.

05

Long time horizons

We are building soil that will be better in ten years than it is now. We optimize for that, not for this season's yield.

How we decide

Governance

40 people with strong opinions require a structure that can handle real disagreement without avoiding it or destroying the community. We use a modified sociocratic consent model.

Major decisions — land use changes, significant capital expenditure, new member acceptance, changes to the mission — require consent from all circle members. Consent means "I can work with this" rather than "I love this." We distinguish explicitly between consent and enthusiasm. You can consent to a decision you disagree with if you believe the process was fair and you'll be heard if problems emerge.
We operate in six working circles: Food Production, Technical Systems, Energy & Infrastructure, Community & Governance, Documentation & Outreach, and Finance. Each circle has operational authority over its domain and a designated link person who participates in full community coordination meetings.
Three-stage process: direct conversation between parties, facilitated conversation with a designated community mediator (rotating role), and full circle review if earlier stages don't resolve. We have used all three stages. Stage three is rare and difficult, but it has worked.
Membership is open to people who want to live on the property full-time, contribute meaningfully to farm operations, participate in governance, and commit to at least one full growing season. We accept new members in cohorts of 2–4 per year, typically in spring. Prospective members spend 2 weeks on the property before any decisions are made by either side.
Technical decisions within a circle's domain (which sensor firmware to run, what crop to trial) are made by the relevant circle without full community input. Decisions that affect community labor, change the nature of members' work, or have significant cost implications require the full process. We learned to draw this line from getting it wrong in both directions.

Applications

Join the Collective

We accept 2–4 new members per year. We are not looking for any particular technical background — we are looking for people who are serious, curious, willing to be wrong, and genuinely interested in spending time outdoors doing physical work alongside intellectual work.

What we have turned away applicants for: an interest in the outputs of the project without willingness to participate in the day-to-day; technical expertise offered in exchange for avoiding non-technical work; an expectation of being an employee rather than a member.
